Driving Internet Access in Thailand: Infrastructure, Strategic Opportunities, and Challenges in the Digital Age

By Songkiat Panmart • Published in Applied Telecommunications & Development Economics • Digital Transformation Monograph Series
Executive Abstract

Telecommunications infrastructure constitutes the foundational general-purpose technology (GPT) of the twenty-first-century global economy. In Thailand, high-speed internet connectivity has catalyzed an explosive expansion of digital financial services, electronic commerce, and remote public services under the national "Thailand 4.0" development mandate. However, this macro-level success conceals complex structural frictions. This research evaluates the economics of internet expansion across Thailand, analyzing the physical deployment of fiber and 5G networks, the urban-rural digital divide, the allocative efficiency of Universal Service Obligation (USO) funds, the competition dynamics following the historic merger of True Corporation and Dtac under the National Broadcasting and Telecommunications Commission (NBTC), and the resulting network externalities on national labor productivity.

1. Connectivity as a General-Purpose Technology (GPT)

Economists define a General-Purpose Technology (GPT)—analogous to the steam engine, the electrical grid, and the internal combustion engine—as an innovation characterized by pervasiveness across diverse industrial sectors, continuous technological improvement, and the capacity to generate complementary innovations (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg, 1995). High-speed broadband internet satisfies each of these criteria. Rather than functioning solely as an end-user consumer utility, connectivity transforms the macroeconomic aggregate production function:

Augmented Aggregate Production Model
$$Y_t = A(K_D, \Omega) \cdot F(K_T, L_t, H_t)$$
Where aggregate real output $Y_t$ depends not merely on physical non-digital capital $K_T$, raw labor $L_t$, and human capital $H_t$, but is magnified by total factor productivity $A$, which is an endogenous function of digital capital stock $K_D$ (fiber optics, spectrum, data centers) and the network connectivity index $\Omega$.

In developing and emerging markets, empirical cross-country econometric estimations by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) and the World Bank suggest that every 10% increase in mobile broadband penetration yields an average boost of 1.2% to 1.8% in gross domestic product (GDP) per capita. For Thailand, whose economic growth rate decelerated to 2.0%–2.5% annually over the preceding decade due to an aging demographic profile, expanding digital capital offers a potent mechanism to overcome the middle-income trap.

2. Thailand's Telecommunications Landscape & Physical Architecture

Thailand’s digital connectivity metrics present an impressive headline narrative. With a population of approximately 71 million, mobile cellular subscriptions exceed 130% penetration. Furthermore, Thailand emerged as an early pioneer within the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) in auctioning 5G spectrum across low (700 MHz), mid (2600 MHz), and high (26 GHz) frequency bands, achieving widespread 5G standalone (SA) population coverage across major metropolitan agglomerations.

On the fixed broadband front, aggressive price competition among internet service providers (ISPs) propelled Fiber-to-the-Home (FTTH) adoption, delivering gigabit-tier speeds at retail prices among the most competitive in the world on a purchasing-power-parity (PPP) basis. Internationally, Thailand serves as a pivotal regional data transit hub, interconnected via undersea submarine communications cable landing stations along the Eastern Seaboard (Rayong, Chonburi) and Southern peninsular corridors (Songkhla, Satun).

However, this aggregate infrastructure exhibits significant asymmetric geographic distribution. Physical fiber routing, high-capacity carrier-neutral data centers, and 5G base station densities remain heavily clustered around the Greater Bangkok Metropolitan Area (BMR) and the Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC), leaving peripheral agricultural and border provinces structurally reliant on lower-throughput backhaul connections.

3. The Dual Digital Divide: First-Degree vs. Second-Degree Disparities

In contemporary telecommunication economics, analytical rigor requires distinguishing between two distinct stages of the digital divide:

  • First-Degree Divide (Infrastructural Access): The physical availability of high-speed telecommunications infrastructure, measured by fiber line presence, cellular signal strength, latency, and affordability of bandwidth.
  • Second-Degree Divide (Capability and Utilization): The human capital, computational literacy, and economic ability to leverage internet connectivity for productive wealth generation, human capital accumulation, and formal financial engagement, rather than solely passive media consumption.

While first-degree access has narrowed significantly due to state-sponsored rural connectivity initiatives, the second-degree divide remains stark. In Bangkok and urban Chiang Mai, micro-enterprises and freelancers leverage cloud computing, software-as-a-service (SaaS) tools, cross-border digital marketing, and automated inventory systems. In contrast, in remote rural communities across Mae Hong Son, Si Sa Ket, or Narathiwat, broadband utilization concentrates disproportionately in social entertainment and digital consumption, offering negligible contribution to rural household balance sheets or agricultural total factor productivity.

4. Evaluating Public Intervention: Net Pracharat and USO Economics

Because remote rural broadband deployment involves high fixed capital expenditures ($\text{CapEx}$) and low anticipated average revenue per user ($\text{ARPU}$), private telecommunications operators face negative net present values ($\text{NPV} < 0$) in sparsely populated locales—a classic market failure in network industries.

To correct this under-provision, the Royal Thai Government deployed the Universal Service Obligation (USO) Fund, administered by the NBTC through mandatory revenue levies on telecommunications licensees, alongside the Ministry of Digital Economy and Society's (MDES) Net Pracharat (Village Broadband Internet) Project. Between 2017 and 2022, Net Pracharat installed public fiber networks and free community Wi-Fi access points across more than 24,700 remote villages nationwide.

Public Scheme Financing Structure Key Operational Achievements Economic Inefficiencies / Pitfalls
Net Pracharat (MDES / NT) Central government budget allocation executed via National Telecom (NT). Installed backbone optical fiber across 24,700+ remote villages; established public Wi-Fi hotspots at village centers. Inadequate operating expenditure (OpEx) for maintenance; equipment breakdown leaves hotspots inactive; poor signal reach beyond village halls.
USO 2.0 / USO Net (NBTC) 3.75% (later reduced to 2.5%) levy on licensed telecommunications operator revenues. Equipped remote schools, public health stations, and established USO Digital Learning Centers in border regions. Bureaucratic delays in fund disbursement; duplicative infrastructure overlapping with Net Pracharat lines; low pedagogical training for center facilitators.

The fundamental economic lesson of Thailand's rural broadband interventions is that subsidizing capital infrastructure (supply-side intervention) without concurrent investments in operational maintenance, local content creation, and digital entrepreneurship training (demand-side stimulation) generates low economic returns on public capital.

5. Market Concentration: The True-Dtac Merger and NBTC Regulation

The competitive structure of Thailand’s mobile telecommunications sector underwent a historic transformation following the merger between True Corporation and Total Access Communication (Dtac). The mobile market had operated as a tripartite oligopoly comprising Advanced Info Service (AIS), True Move H, and Dtac. The amalgamation created a duopolistic market structure dominated by AIS and the merged True-Dtac entity, with state-owned National Telecom (NT) holding a marginal market share.

Antitrust Economics & The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI): Before the merger, the Thai mobile market’s HHI stood at approximately 3,700 points—already categorized as highly concentrated under international antitrust benchmarks. Following the consolidation, the post-merger HHI surged past 4,800 points, an increase exceeding 1,000 points. Economic theory (Stigler, 1964; Tirole, 1988) predicts that tight duopolies elevate the risk of tacit collusion, price coordination, and diminished incentives for continuous capital expenditure in coverage expansion.

In response, the NBTC enacted structural remedy conditions, including retail price ceilings, mandatory Mobile Virtual Network Operator (MVNO) wholesale capacity leasing, accounting separation of mobile brands, and stringent Quality of Service (QoS) benchmarks. Nonetheless, independent consumer monitoring groups documented subsequent elimination of low-cost unlimited promotional data packages, effectively increasing the real per-gigabyte connectivity expense for low-income consumers.

Preserving consumer welfare in this concentrated market requires the NBTC to aggressively foster the development of independent MVNOs by mandating wholesale bandwidth tariffs pegged strictly to Long-Run Incremental Cost (LRIC) models, thereby introducing competitive retail price tension into the duopolistic landscape.

6. Network Externalities: PromptPay, FinTech, and Micro-Enterprises

The profound macroeconomic dividend of widespread smartphone and mobile broadband penetration in Thailand is manifested in its digital payment ecosystem. Under the Bank of Thailand's National e-Payment Master Plan, the implementation of PromptPay—a national interoperable real-time retail payment system based on proxy IDs (phone numbers and national citizen IDs)—revolutionized financial intermediation.

PromptPay slashed transaction fees to zero for retail consumers, overcoming the traditional credit card interchange tax and physical cash handling costs. The macroeconomic effects are manifold:

  1. Drastic Reduction in Cash Frictions: Transaction settlement velocity surged, allowing capital to circulate through the informal economy at negligible friction. Thailand consistently ranks among the top three nations globally in real-time digital transaction volume per capita.
  2. Inclusion of the Informal Micro-Merchant Sector: Street vendors, motorcycle taxi operators, and informal market stalls seamlessly integrated into the financial system, generating verifiable transaction data. This digital footprint serves as alternative credit scoring data, unlocking formal micro-credit and diminishing reliance on predatory loan sharks charging interest rates exceeding 20% per month.
  3. Efficient Delivery of Fiscal Counter-Cyclical Stimulus: During macroeconomic shocks (such as the COVID-19 pandemic and inflationary energy crises), the state leveraged digital platforms (e.g., the "Pao Tang" digital wallet) to distribute targeted fiscal stimulus (co-payment subsidies) directly to tens of millions of citizens within hours, bypassing bureaucratic leakage and corruption.

7. Strategic Policy Roadmap for Equitable Digital Growth

To transform digital access into an enduring locomotive of inclusive economic prosperity, Thai economic policymakers must execute a multi-pillar strategic agenda:

Strategic Pillar Key Policy Action Economic Target
Infrastructure Sharing & Open Access Mandate open-access passive and active infrastructure sharing (fiber backhaul, tower assets) for rural corridors. Prevent wasteful duplication of capital expenditure; lower entry barriers for local community ISPs.
Spectrum Allocation Reform Reorient spectrum auctions from state revenue maximization toward coverage commitments and regional rollouts. Accelerate 5G deployment across non-metropolitan manufacturing, logistics, and precision agricultural clusters.
MVNO Market Revitalization Enforce cost-oriented wholesale price controls on duopoly network owners under NBTC regulatory scrutiny. Restore consumer price competition and safeguard affordable mobile data bundles for low-income segments.
Digital Capability Augmentation Repurpose 30% of annual USO funding into localized digital entrepreneurship and agricultural IoT training. Bridge the second-degree digital divide; convert passive media consumption into productive household income.

By shifting national policy focus from pure physical wire installation to rigorous competition protection and demand-side capability building, Thailand can ensure that its telecommunications revolution serves as a truly democratic foundation for sustainable, long-term economic growth.
